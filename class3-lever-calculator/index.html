Methodology & Assumptions

Bent-lever geometry

The lever is a rigid body: fulcrum F, effort point E, load point P, with |FE|=L₁, |EP|=L₂, and interior bend angle β at E. Because the body is rigid, the fulcrum-to-load distance R is fixed regardless of rotation (law of cosines):

R = √( L₁² + L₂² − 2·L₁·L₂·cos β )

A straight lever is the limiting case β = 180°, where R = L₁ + L₂. Bending the arm at E shortens R relative to a straight lever of the same segment lengths — this is the effect you're compensating for by measuring L₂ from the effort point rather than assuming L₁+L₂ is the load arm.

Static force ratio

Torque balance about the fulcrum, assuming both forces act perpendicular to their respective arms (idealized, worst-case-for-force / best-case-for-motion assumption typical of lever hand calcs):

F_load = F_effort · (L₁ / R)

Class-3 behavior (L₁ < R) trades force for speed: the load point moves farther and faster than the effort point for the same rotation, which is what lets a small, fast effort-mass impact drive a heavier load mass into the spring.

Effort-side stroke (work-energy)

Given an incoming effort mass and velocity, the kinetic energy that must be absorbed while holding the effort force at its design value sets the minimum effort-side travel:

KE_in = ½·m_e·v_e² s_e(min) = KE_in / F_effort Δθ = s_e / L₁   (rotation from hard stop to full extension)

Raising the allowable design force F_effort is the only way to shrink s_e for a given impact energy — this is the direct answer to "how little can the effort mass move." The max lever rotation field (card 01) is your actual mechanical ceiling on Δθ — the status panel flags a design that needs more rotation than the hardware physically has room for.

Load handoff & spring sizing

An efficiency factor η lumps pivot friction, lever inertia, and bearing drag. Energy reaching the load mass at the moment the lever hits full extension:

v_l = √( 2·η·KE_in / m_l )

From there the load mass is treated as a free mass entering a linear spring (simple harmonic energy balance), giving the spring stroke you need to spec:

x_max = v_l · √(m_l / k) F_spring,max = k · x_max t_spring = (π/2)·√(m_l / k)

Total linear-bearing length to budget for is the load-side arc travel during the lever's powered stroke, plus x_max.

Return stroke & the dead-center technique

The same spring pushes both masses back toward the home hard stop — without help, they arrive with roughly the energy they left with, which is the "hammer on anvil" ringing. The fix models both masses on their own linear bearing, pinned to the lever through a slotted (Scotch-yoke) coupling. A crank pin at radius r moving through angle φ (measured from where the arm is parallel to the slide axis) has slide-axis velocity v = r·ω·sin(φ) — zero at φ=0, the mechanical "dead center." Mount each bearing axis parallel to its arm at the home position (α=0) and that mass's return velocity vanishes right at contact, no matter the leftover energy:

E_return = η² · KE_in (round trip: through the lever, spring, and back) I_eff = I_lever + m_e·L1²·sin²(α_e) + m_l·R²·sin²(α_l) ω_home = √( 2·E_return / I_eff ) v_e,home = L1·ω_home·sin(α_e) v_l,home = R·ω_home·sin(α_l)

At α_e = α_l = 0, both v_e,home and v_l,home are exactly zero — all of E_return converts into spinning the lever itself, which is a far smaller thing to arrest than two heavy translating masses. This is a purely geometric answer, not a damping one: no buffer material required. The catch is that exact dead center also means zero torque at that same point, so the forward stroke needs a small deliberate offset (or an off-axis component in the incoming impact) to break away reliably — α_e and α_l let you dial in exactly how much residual return velocity that reliability margin costs.

Note on the diagram's layout: the schematic draws the effort and load sliders on one common horizontal rail (spring/rail on the left, effort force arriving from the right, in line with that rail) for a compact, easy-to-read packaging view. Individually, α_e=0 and α_l=0 are each independently well-defined regardless of layout. Forcing the two sliders onto one truly common straight rail while also holding both α's at exactly 0° simultaneously is only geometrically exact for a straight lever (β=180°) — for a genuinely bent lever, a real build on a single common rail will have a small minimum combined offset baked in by β. Treat the diagram as the packaging concept; verify the exact dead-center geometry for your specific rail arrangement in CAD.

Sensitivity across testing bounds

For each parameter with a lower/upper bound filled in (card 05), the tool re-runs the physics with just that one parameter swapped to its bound value — everything else held at its primary value — and reports the resulting range for the key outputs. This is a standard one-at-a-time ("tornado") sensitivity check: it tells you which uncertain input actually matters, not a worst-case combination of all of them at once (for that, sweep the full space in the Design Space Explorer).

This is a lumped-parameter hand-calculation intended for scoping bench-test hardware (spring rate, stroke, arm lengths) — not a certified dynamic simulation. Arc travel on the load side is a first-order approximation of the true slider/connecting-link path; the return-stroke section assumes a slotted/Scotch-yoke coupling specifically (a simple connecting-rod link will behave similarly near dead center but not identically). Verify against a CAD motion study or prototype once β, the rotation range, or the offset angles are large.
